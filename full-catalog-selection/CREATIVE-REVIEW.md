# Creative review: full-catalog selection and original themes

Review date: 2026-09-23. Inputs: all 14 requests and captured handoffs in `briefs.json`; `pilot-01` report and interpretation; all 14 `creative-01` decisions; direct visual inspection of Story Map, Marginalia, and Lecture Hall preview sheets. No generated output decks existed at review time. Statements about other candidates are critiques of the selector's recorded evidence, not independent visual certification.

This is the delegated file-only analysis role from `writer-eval`. Its usual writer-result metrics do not apply yet: there are no paired rendered decks, retention readings, or blinded judge tallies. No paid calls were run by this reviewer.

## What changed, and what remains weak

The first pass chose the library for 14/14 briefs. The creativity pass chooses it for 10/14, with original directions for pirate cosmology, the authentication portal, media history, and boutique wellness. It corrects both obvious initial failures: children's Story Map for a pirate, and restrained Pipeline for the explicitly glassy, dimensional authentication demo.

That is routing progress, not evidence of better decks. In particular, the original pirate direction still suggests a whimsical ship, parchment, brass, rope, compass, and flags. That can become a stock pirate costume placed over corporate slides. The opportunity is more ambitious: make celestial navigation an explanatory language for cosmology, aimed at an adult imagined audience. No children's illustration default; no schoolroom worksheets.

The selector still rewards descriptions that sound purposeful. “Authored world,” “premium,” “editorial,” and “evidence-led” do not establish an exceptional result. Marginalia is now selected for both Hegel and Vietnamese literature: appropriate typography, but the generic annotated-reading system may erase what makes those subjects different. Weather Watchers is adjacent to ozone by sky imagery, not necessarily by explanatory capability. These are useful challenger cases even when the library decision is defensible.

Conversely, the media-history decision may be overcorrecting. The original proposes three palettes and changing graphic languages, but a strong fixed editorial grid can tell that story beautifully through content. Do not reward “original” merely because its rationale describes a concept more eloquently. Compare the finished decks.

## Creative direction and risks for every original-theme deck

All 14 should receive an original-theme arm on Brand Base Standard, including cases where the selector prefers the library. Hold the content plan and writer settings fixed within a pair. Below are creative opportunities, not prewritten slide content or a required composition inventory.

### 1. Ozone for ten-year-olds — “The invisible shield club”

Make an invisible phenomenon visible through a recurring Earth cutaway and two distinct kinds of sunlight paths. Crisp blue atmosphere, sun-yellow signals, generous white space, friendly expressive linework; one consistent scale model throughout. The visual payoff should be a before/after scientific explanation a child can retell, with a short predict-and-reveal activity.

**Avoid:** a smiling planet pasted next to bullet lists; weather charts that imply ozone depletion is a weather event; conflating climate change, ground-level ozone, and stratospheric protection. Verify age-appropriate actions instead of implying generic recycling directly repairs ozone. Do not use frightening catastrophe imagery or preschool typography for ten-year-olds.

**Signature test:** can a child point to a diagram and explain what the protective layer changes?

### 2. Ozone and investment policy — “The exposure atlas”

Build a sober portfolio atlas: charcoal ink, mineral white, a tightly controlled exposure color. A repeated exposure-to-action structure connects policy drivers, affected operations, transition choices, and committee decisions. Originality lives in one lucid visual argument, not in satellite photos. A meaningful matrix or scenario bridge should carry more weight than the cover.

**Avoid:** invented portfolio holdings, unsupported returns, current policy assertions without verified sources, and sustainability clip art. Distinguish illustrative scenarios from real committee data. Keep ozone-depleting substances, refrigerant transitions, and climate-related policy relationships precise.

**Signature test:** can the committee identify the action and the evidence/assumptions behind it in one scan?

### 3. Salon lookbook — “Cut / movement / texture”

Treat hair as sculptural material. Develop a photographic direction built around silhouette, close-up texture, and motion; vary full-image spreads and tightly cropped paired studies with spare captions. A warm gallery-white system and one assertive typographic voice should frame the imagery rather than compete. Distinct hairstyles need distinctly useful views.

**Avoid:** image placeholders in the final creativity assessment; generic beauty portraits where hair is obscured; generated examples represented as the actual salon's work; inconsistent models or lighting that make the lookbook feel like unrelated stock. Use supplied salon images if available; otherwise label concept imagery accurately in the report.

**Signature test:** does each image expose a specific cut, texture, or styling choice, and does the sequence feel art-directed?

### 4. QBR — “A quarter in motion”

Use a single recurring visual grammar that connects outcome → drivers → intervention → next quarter. Exact, spacious typography; white and near-black with one signal color; disciplined charts and a few decisive large figures. Create a memorable driver-to-decision diagram instead of introducing an arbitrary motif.

**Avoid:** fabricated results presented as company facts, faux precision, chart axes without units, generic glossy abstract objects, and decorative data dashboards. With no company data supplied, mark every sample metric consistently and visibly.

**Signature test:** does the deck make the performance explanation and requested leadership decisions unmistakable?

### 5. Dark creative conference — “Afterimage”

Explore dramatic light, silhouette, and typographic cropping as a deliberate visual argument. Establish one signature contrast between monumental imagery and short, spatially composed statements; quiet slides make the peaks matter. Use near-black as a field, not a reason to fill every slide with neon gradients. The dark constraint applies across the deck.

**Avoid:** inventing a speaker's thesis or achievements and presenting them as supplied facts. The brief lacks a topic: use an explicitly recorded provisional topic, shared across both arms. Do not let the handoff's 55-word target turn live slides into scripts; that target is subordinate to the user's live dramatic presentation goal.

**Signature test:** are there two or three genuinely memorable stage-scale moments with purposeful pacing between them?

### 6. Boutique wellness — “The restorative hour”

Make the guest's day the organizing idea: small physical rituals, staff choreography, and property value are successive views of the same experience. Crisp white, translucent soft teal, deep teal text; natural light and tactile materials. A recurring visual path can connect guest benefits to operations and the partnership ask without reducing hospitality to a corporate funnel.

**Avoid:** sage or beige replacing the requested white and teal; generic spa imagery; invented ROI or medical outcomes; a beautiful retreat brochure with no sales proposition. Any commercial estimates need explicit scenario assumptions.

**Signature test:** does a hotel manager see both the experience they could offer and the operational decision required?

### 7. Detailed handover — “The project field guide”

Create a durable operational document with a visible navigation spine, clear section identity, ownership labels, decision records, and a readable system map. Warm white, graphite, one precise operational accent. Design makes dependencies and responsibilities discoverable; this is an opportunity for exceptional information design.

**Avoid:** stripping out necessary detail to satisfy a wow target; giant decorative headings; tiny footnotes; fictional owners/statuses presented as real; ornamental illustration consuming reference space. No project data was supplied, so label example content or placeholders honestly and consistently.

**Signature test:** can a new owner find an unresolved decision, its owner, and the next action without the presenter?

### 8. Big Bang for a pirate — “The navigator's atlas of an expanding sea”

Build an adult celestial-navigation atlas: abyssal blue, ivory engraved labels, sparing oxidized brass, luminous spectral traces. The essential visual is the changing distance between charted landmarks as the underlying coordinate field expands. A redshift plate turns stretched light into evidence; a cosmic timeline reads like an expedition log. Nautical phrasing should add wit and familiarity while the diagrams do the explanation. A ship can serve as a tiny navigation marker, not a mascot or the centerpiece of every slide.

**Avoid:** children's faces, cartoon pirates, treasure chests, ropes around cards, parchment wallpaper, or decorative constellations standing in for explanation. Do not imply an explosion from a location into pre-existing empty space, a center/edge the pirate could sail to, or certainty about what preceded the observable early universe. Metaphor limitations should be explicit where necessary. “Before there were stars” is a safer teaching frame than inventing “before the universe.”

**Signature test:** do expansion, cooling, and observational evidence become memorable because of the navigation concept, while remaining scientifically defensible?

### 9. Authentication portal — “Inside one secure request”

Use the user's exact visual language: near-black developer dashboard, frosted panels, subtle cyan/violet edge light, dimensional architecture, credible browser UI. Follow one sign-in request through the system so arrows, code, request/response cards, and status changes form an explanation. Seven slides exactly. Theme and assets should feel like one designed software product.

**Avoid:** seven vague cybersecurity slides; decorative shields without concrete controls; code drawn inside generated raster images; tiny code; contradictory route names or payloads; plaintext passwords in storage; secrets/tokens displayed as real credentials. State any assumptions about sessions/JWTs consistently. Live presenters need concise snippets and readable labels, not full files.

**Signature test:** can someone trace input → validation → authentication → response and understand what prevents failure/abuse?

### 10. Media history — “The grid learns to move”

Preserve a single strong editorial grid and let one visual object transform across print, digital, and computational eras. Selected artifact images, side-by-side interface structures, and a continuous timeline make the evolution concrete. Typographic texture may change subtly, but baseline, hierarchy, and annotation conventions should remain stable. Restraint is part of the explicit brief.

**Avoid:** three disconnected template looks; arbitrary neon computational imagery; visual claims of inevitable progress; unverified historical dates or invented archival examples. Do not present generated reconstructions as authentic historical artifacts. The history should include continuities and trade-offs as well as new capabilities.

**Signature test:** does the visual structure itself reveal what changed about production, distribution, interaction, or authorship?

### 11. Ragamuffin War for children — “Objects from a changing place”

Use a bright illustrated museum-table concept: recognizable everyday objects open each historical question; simple maps locate events; a timeline and recurring human characters connect the sequence. Clear cut-paper shapes and warm colors can feel inventive without violent imagery. A quiz should ask children to connect causes and experiences, not merely remember dates.

**Avoid:** battle scenes; reducing a war to cheerful adventure; generic European castles/pirates; misleading maps or invented costumes; flattening contested histories into good and bad teams. Verify dates, places, and representations before final output. Vocabulary must match a children's audience without erasing meaningful context.

**Signature test:** can a child locate the story, explain an age-appropriate cause, and remember how everyday lives were affected?

### 12. Hegel — “Ideas in motion”

Treat philosophical explanation as progressive visual transformation. One concrete example can recur as relations and perspectives change; pair generous text with a small number of precise concept diagrams. Warm ivory and ink with one deep accent, intellectually inviting typography, and a recurring visual question. The signature should be understanding emerging over successive slides.

**Avoid:** a rigid thesis–antithesis–synthesis machine presented as an adequate account of Hegel; decorative statues; misleading flowcharts that make contested concepts look mechanical; fabricated quotations. Clearly distinguish paraphrase, teaching simplification, and sourced quotation. An elegant quotation layout does not by itself explain an abstract idea.

**Signature test:** can a learner explain one hard concept using the deck's worked example rather than repeating a label?

### 13. Agricultural machinery — “One field, several decisions”

Use a technical field workbook: strict white background, red annotations, strong black text, precise equipment silhouettes and overhead field diagrams. Follow one transparent example from selection and usage through time, capacity, cost, and improvement choices. A before/after field-operation diagram offers useful visual drama while respecting practical training.

**Avoid:** an unrelated tractor hero plus generic bullets; color drift into green/orange; inconsistent units; calculations with hidden assumptions; fabricated source-document content. The requested document is absent from the captured input: explicitly label the prototype as a created illustrative example, not a faithful conversion of an unseen document.

**Signature test:** can the audience reproduce one calculation and see why a specific equipment or operational choice changes it?

### 14. The Tale of Kieu — “A poem carried by its rhythm”

Build a literary reading room with restrained ivory paper, ink, a muted vermilion accent, elegant highly legible serif typography, and Vietnamese-specific imagery. Make the six/eight syllable structure of lục bát visible using carefully verified text and clear annotation; connect a character's choices with the work's humanitarian reading. White space and subtle linework should preserve projection readability.

**Avoid:** generic pan-Asian styling, Japanese brush conventions treated as Vietnamese identity, tiny manuscript body text, lost Vietnamese diacritics, invented quotations, or English translation lines presented as if they preserve Vietnamese syllabic structure. Verify source text, translation attribution, names, and literary interpretation. Cultural specificity must be researched, not inferred from an ink-wash aesthetic.

**Signature test:** does the audience understand something about the poem's form and Thúy Kiều's experience that a generic literary template would not reveal?

## Practical output rubric

Judge actual rendered decks blind to the route, in both presentation orders. Cite slide numbers and concrete visual evidence; eloquent design-direction prose earns no credit. Use all 14 cases, not only the pirate success. If order reverses the preference, record the pair as inconclusive.

First apply hard checks: required slide count and palette; audience/format; readable and unclipped content; no missing required subject; unsupported factual numbers/quotations clearly flagged; no harmful scientific, technical, historical, or cultural distortion. A hard failure blocks “ready,” regardless of visual score. Separate generation defects from prompt/selection defects.

Score each dimension 1–5, with evidence:

| Dimension | Weight | 1 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| Creative specificity / memorable idea | 25% | Generic skin or stock motif | Some tailored moments | Distinct, brief-specific visual premise realized across the deck |
| Useful visual storytelling | 25% | Decoration beside prose | Some explanatory visuals | Composition and visuals reveal relationships, sequence, or evidence the audience would otherwise miss |
| Audience and task fit | 15% | Wrong age/register/use | Serviceable | Precisely suited to how this audience will use and understand it |
| Coherence and pacing | 15% | Unrelated slides or repeated monotony | Consistent but flat | Recognizable system with purposeful variation and memorable peaks |
| Readability and craft | 15% | Clipping, small text, weak contrast | Readable with rough edges | Strong hierarchy, reliable spacing, polished details, clear projection/reference reading |
| Content integrity | 5% + hard gate | Unsupported/misleading | Mostly correct with minor gaps | Accurate, well-scoped, explicit assumptions, faithful coverage |

Report a separate **wow verdict**: absent / occasional / sustained. “Sustained” requires at least one exceptional substantive visual, a memorable opening, and convincing craft through the middle and ending. More colors, images, effects, diagrams, or layout families do not automatically increase the grade. Business/reference decks can wow through exceptional clarity.

The weighted score is an aid, not the verdict. Also ask: Which deck would you give this user? What one change would most improve the loser? Did the original arm earn its additional complexity? Does the winner actually exceed competent default quality?

## Iteration recommendations

1. Generate every original arm; keep the initial versions and their design briefs immutable as baseline evidence.
2. Use real images for creative assessment, especially salon, conference, wellness, and pirate. Grey stand-ins cannot settle those cases. If using writer-bench stand-ins, label those results as composition-only; the bench also does not test the production finisher or planner.
3. Grade paired decks across all 14. Identify failures by source: theme schema/rendering, constrained base layouts, weak art direction, writing, missing assets, or incorrect selection. A theme cannot supply every diagram/UI layout by itself.
4. Revise the worst original outputs, plus at least two cases where the library wins. Keep the content plan fixed unless the content plan caused the failure; record any such change so it is not misattributed to theming.
5. For pirate, authentication, and culturally specific literature, judge the result against the actual requested experience, not just the library alternative. Winning a weak baseline is insufficient.
6. Inspect cross-deck sameness: if originals repeatedly use identical hero-left/text-right cards, three rounded boxes, identical image prompting, or the same typography pair, the prototype is recoloring a base rather than demonstrating ambitious authored design.
7. Repeat promising selection behavior rather than trusting one pass. A successful prototype need not hit a target original percentage, but must reliably refuse the pirate mismatch and honor explicit visual constraints.

**Verdict:** retain the creativity prompt as a promising routing revision, not a finished solution. The largest next gain should come from designing and rendering coherent, content-specific original decks—not adding more adjectives to the selector. The strongest counter-evidence to an original-first preference remains QBR, handover, salon, and technical agriculture, where the library has credible structural advantages and original themes must prove real added value.
